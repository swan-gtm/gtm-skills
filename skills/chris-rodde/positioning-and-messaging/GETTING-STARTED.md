## What this skill does

It runs positioning and messaging work as a four-stage sequence drawn from four practitioners: Bob Moesta (demand evidence), April Dunford (the positioning decision), Anthony Pierri and Rob Kaminski of Fletch PMM (expression), and Andy Raskin (strategic narrative).

The output is a single artifact — a messaging framework document in three layers — that a salesperson can open and use without having attended any workshop.

**The sequence is the method.** Evidence, then decision, then words, then story. Most bad messaging is not a writing problem; it is an undecided or unevidenced positioning problem being covered with adjectives. Teams that start with the story end up with several competing descriptions of the same product and no differentiation that survives a competitor's feature page.

Use it for a new framework, a rewrite, a launch, a homepage, or an audit of what you already have.

## What you need before you start

### Essential

**1. Recorded customer conversations — 6 to 12, transcribed.**

This is the single highest-value input and the one most teams underestimate. Without it, the skill can still run, but Stage 0 becomes guesswork and everything downstream inherits that.

Where these usually live: your call-recording or meeting-notes tool, or your CRM.

**Choose them deliberately. Weight toward losses.**

| Type | How many | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Recent wins | 2–3 | Push and pull — what made them move |
| Losses | 3–4 | Where anxiety and habit live |
| Stalls / no-decision | 3–4 | The richest and most neglected source |
| Churned customers | 1–2 if available | The forces running in reverse |

Most teams read only their wins. The wins tell you why people who already agreed with you agreed with you. The stalls tell you what is actually blocking revenue.

**2. Access to your competitors' live marketing.**

The skill runs a table-stakes test against what competitors claim *right now*, not what you remember them claiming. Claude needs web search enabled for this. If it isn't, have the competitor homepages and product pages ready to paste.

Name your real competitors, including the free or bundled capability inside a tool your buyers already own. That is usually the one that matters.

**3. Your existing materials.**

Homepage copy, current sales decks, any messaging document or brand guide, one-pagers. Include the dates they were last edited — vintage explains most contradictions, and a stale asset is a different problem from a wrong one.

**4. Your loss and stall reasons.**

Whatever your CRM records, however messy. Patterns across ten opportunities are more reliable than any single conversation.

### Strongly recommended

- **Review site content** — your own and competitors' third-party review-site pages. Unfiltered customer language, free, no interviews required.
- **Support tickets and community posts** — how customers describe problems when they aren't talking to a vendor.
- **Sales objection log** — even an informal one. If it doesn't exist, ask three reps for their five most common objections.
- **Search and analytics data** — the terms people actually use to find you. Directly useful for the category decision.
- **Win/loss interview notes**, if you run them.

### Useful context to state up front

- **Scope** — whole company, one product, or one segment? Portfolio positioning is a harder, different exercise.
- **Constraints** — runway, team size, whether product-market fit is proven. These change what positioning is even available to you. A company with two years of runway can attempt things a company with nine months cannot.
- **Who decides** — positioning that marketing writes alone does not stick.

## If you don't have transcripts

Common, and not fatal. In rough order of value:

1. **Run five switch interviews now.** Call customers who bought in the last 90 days and reconstruct the timeline of their decision. The question bank is in `references/moesta-demand.md`. Five good interviews beat fifty survey responses.
2. **Mine review sites.** Yours and your competitors'. Look for the sentences describing the situation *before* purchase.
3. **Debrief your sales team.** Not "what do customers want" — ask them to narrate three specific recent deals, one won, one lost, one stalled, in detail.
4. **Read support tickets and community threads** for problem language.
5. **Talk to your two or three most engaged customers** and ask what they were doing before you.

Tell Claude which of these you used. The skill will mark the evidence as weaker and say so in the final document rather than presenting assumptions as findings.

## How to prepare your inputs

- **Give full transcripts, not summaries.** Summaries strip the exact words, and the exact words are the point. Verbatim language is what keeps house vocabulary out of your website.
- **Label each one**: date, account type (not necessarily the name), outcome (won / lost / stalled / churned), and who was in the room.
- **Anonymize if you need to** — "a mid-market logistics company" works fine. The method doesn't need identities.
- **Don't clean them up.** Filler, tangents and interruptions are harmless. Paraphrasing is not.
- **Note the format.** Sales calls are not switch interviews: your team is talking, and the customer is reacting to a pitch. Say so, and the skill will treat push and pull as partly contaminated rather than as validated findings.

## How to start

Just describe the work. The skill triggers on the task, not on a keyword:

- "Help me rebuild our messaging framework."
- "Our positioning isn't landing — nobody can explain what we do."
- "Review our homepage and decks against our actual differentiation."
- "We're launching a new product and need positioning for it."

Then attach or paste your inputs. If you have a lot of material, start with the transcripts and add the rest as you reach each stage.

**If you already have a framework or a website**, say so first. The skill runs an audit before anything else, because inherited language is the main source of drift and you need to know what you're inheriting.

## What each stage costs you

Rough guide for a single product with decent inputs:

| Stage | Your time | Mostly spent on |
|---|---|---|
| Audit (if applicable) | 1 session | Gathering existing materials |
| 0 — Evidence | 2–4 sessions | Supplying and discussing transcripts |
| 1 — Decision | 2–3 sessions | Arguing about competitive alternatives. This is where the real work is |
| 2 — Expression | 2–4 sessions | Drafting and testing copy |
| 3 — Narrative | 1–2 sessions | Shaping the deck story |

Stage 1 takes longest and generates the most disagreement. That is the exercise working, not failing. The value-mapping step in particular — laddering each capability to something a budget owner cares about — takes longer than everything around it.

## Who needs to be involved

- **Stage 0** — you and the evidence. Solo work.
- **Stage 1** — bring in sales, product, and whoever owns revenue. The competitive-alternatives and best-fit-customer discussions need people who talk to buyers. A decision made without sales in the room will be ignored by sales.
- **Stage 2** — marketing leads, sales reviews.
- **Stage 3** — the CEO should own the narrative rather than receive it. Delegated entirely to marketing, it becomes an artifact that circulates and dies.

## What you get

A messaging framework document in three layers, ordered by how often each gets read:

1. **The decision** — problem, competitive alternatives, what only you can claim, who it's for, category, what you are not
2. **The expression** — narrative, copy by surface, proof library, quote bank, objection handling, vocabulary
3. **The evidence** — demand research, competitive scan, sources, change log

In front of all three sits **the one page**: your one-liner, category, segment, three claims, real alternative and strongest proof. Written last, from everything else. It's the part people memorize.

The test of the artifact: can a salesperson who missed the workshop write an on-message email in five minutes?

## Common ways this goes wrong

**Bringing only wins.** The most common mistake. Your losses hold the anxiety and habit data, and those are what kill deals that "went well."

**Wanting to start with the story.** It's the enjoyable part and the worst starting point. The skill will tell you what you're skipping and then do it anyway if you insist — but expect the story to need rewriting once the decision exists.

**Defending a coined category.** If you have an invented platform name, it will be challenged. Category creation needs capital, patience and an evangelism budget. Choosing an existing category with real search demand is usually the stronger play, and the skill will say so.

**Softening copy in review.** Specific copy feels risky — it forecloses segments and makes claims that can be checked. The abstraction almost always creeps back. When it does, run the competitor-swap test again: put a rival's logo on the line and see if it still reads as true.

**Treating the output as finished.** Positioning is a decision under current market conditions. Set a review trigger, not just a review date: *when a major competitor ships or bundles a comparable capability, when the ICP changes, or in six months — whichever comes first.*

**Skipping the stranger test.** The only external check in the whole method, and the one people skip. Show the headline to someone outside your company and ask what the product does. If they can't say, rewrite.

## What this skill will not do

Stated plainly so you can plan around it.

- **It cannot interview your customers.** It analyses what you bring.
- **It cannot validate claims about your own product.** If you tell it a capability is unique, it takes your word — except where a competitor's public marketing contradicts you.
- **It will not settle business decisions for you.** Which segment to target first, whether to lead with the enterprise story or the mid-market one — it will surface the tension, lay out the trade-off and recommend, but the call is yours.
- **It does not distribute the framework.** A document nobody opens has no value. Plan the rollout separately.
- **It is not a substitute for real research.** Treat outputs as strong hypotheses. The structural observations — patterns across many accounts — are more reliable than anything drawn from a single conversation.
