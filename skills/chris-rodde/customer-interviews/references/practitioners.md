## How the five fit together

The brief was customer interviews for marketing in B2B tech. The skill is built on one model with four roles inside it:

- **The model — Moesta & Spiek.** What a switch is: a timeline of events moved by four forces. Every interview reconstructs one; every rubric and report is organized by it.
- **Who and so what — Laudi & Suellentrop.** Which customers to study for marketing, and how findings become messaging and the customer journey.
- **How to ask — Torres.** Story prompts that collect what happened, and the discipline of synthesizing one interview at a time.
- **Question hygiene — Fitzpatrick.** The rules that keep people from politely misleading you.
- **Win-loss operations — Clozd.** Interviewing buyers about competitive decisions: timing, the committee, neutrality, comparability at scale.

Each concept has one owner. Where two practitioners describe the same thing, the skill uses the model's term and lists the aliases in `switch-model.md`.

Honorable mentions worth knowing: Steve Portigal (*Interviewing Users*) for interview craft; Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers) for voice-of-customer copy mining.

## Bob Moesta & Chris Spiek — the model

**Who:** Moesta worked with Clayton Christensen on developing Jobs to be Done theory, founded The Re-Wired Group, and wrote *Demand-Side Sales 101* (with Greg Engle). Chris Spiek, his longtime Re-Wired Group collaborator, developed the switch interview with him; their method is taught through jobstobedone.org. The "mattress interview" in Christensen's *Competing Against Luck* is the method's best-known worked example.

**Core idea:** people don't buy products; they hire them to make progress in a struggling moment, and fire whatever they used before. Demand is created by that moment, not by a demographic or a persona. To understand it, reconstruct what actually happened.

**What it contributes here**
- **The timeline.** First thought → passive looking → active looking → deciding → onboarding → ongoing use, separated by events. The basis of every guide's structure and the "story specificity" anchors in Rubric C.
- **The four forces.** Push and pull drive change; anxiety and habit resist it. A switch happens only when the first pair outweighs the second. Forces surface from events; they aren't asked for directly.
- **Progress in three dimensions.** Functional, emotional and social — the progress someone is trying to make.
- **Pathways.** Clustering interviews by recurring combinations of push and pull, rather than by demographic segment.
- **Switch-interview technique.** Documentary framing ("we're reconstructing what happened; small details matter"); keep asking "and then what happened?"; play dumb; connect the wrong dots so they correct you; treat contradictions as refinements; interview in pairs.
- **Who to interview.** People who moved — recent switchers within roughly 30–90 days, near-switchers, and churned customers — not only happy customers, because the struggle is where the data is.
- **Sample size.** About 10 per round, no more than 12; patterns in the forces usually repeat by the seventh or eighth.

**Limitation to know:** the method is built to understand switching. It says less about how to choose a target market among many, or how to run comparable interviews across a large win-loss program — which is where Laudi & Suellentrop and Clozd come in. There are also two Jobs to be Done schools: Moesta's, centered on the switch and its context, and Tony Ulwick's outcome-driven innovation, centered on measurable outcome statements. This skill uses Moesta's. Note the distinction if a user raises it; don't blend them.

## Georgiana Laudi & Claire Suellentrop — who and so what

**Who:** co-founders of Forget the Funnel (the consultancy now operates as Customer Led) and co-authors of *Forget the Funnel: A Customer-Led Approach for Driving Predictable, Recurring Revenue*. Suellentrop previously led marketing at Calendly and brings a journalism and radio interviewing background. Their method has been used by SaaS companies including Wistia, SparkToro and Appcues.

**Core idea:** the Customer-Led Growth framework — get inside the heads of your best customers, map and measure their experience, then find the biggest growth levers. Interviews are framed explicitly as a marketing tool: reverse-engineer how your best customers found, chose and succeeded with you, so you can find more like them.

**What it contributes here**
- **Best customers, not all customers.** Criteria: happily paying, little hand-holding needed, urgent problem, retention or expansion potential, easy to market to.
- **Survey vs. interviews.** With roughly 500+ ideal customers, start with a survey; fewer, or one method only, choose interviews.
- **Saturation.** 10–12 interviews surface the pattern.
- **A marketer's question set.** Their JTBD interview structure — life before the product, the trigger, the evaluation, onboarding, value now — is the switch timeline at lower resolution. This skill lays their questions on Moesta's six phases. They also publish a template of about 25 questions plus an invite email script.
- **Synthesis into marketing.** Clustering quotes into struggle, motivation and desired-outcome themes (in this skill: push, pull, progress); placing them on an experience map with the milestones a customer must reach; turning the result into a messaging guide.

**Limitation to know:** focused on SaaS and recurring revenue; best-customer sampling deliberately excludes the people who didn't buy, so pair it with win-loss, churn or non-switcher interviews when the decision is about who you're losing.

## Teresa Torres — how to ask

**Who:** product discovery coach, author of *Continuous Discovery Habits*, runs Product Talk and the Story-Based Customer Interviews course (which includes B2B example interviews and an AI interview coach).

**Core idea:** continuous discovery — weekly customer touchpoints — with interviews that collect specific stories rather than asking direct questions.

**What it contributes here**
- **Research questions vs. interview questions.** What you want to learn is not what you ask. Direct questions produce fast, plausible, unreliable answers; story prompts ("tell me about the last time…") produce actual behavior.
- **Scoping.** Match the breadth of the story prompt to the outcome you're working on.
- **The golden rule.** Let the participant talk about what they care about most; don't force a story they don't have.
- **The interview snapshot.** A one-page synthesis of each interview — memorable quote, quick facts, insights, opportunities, experience map, and a photo — done one interview at a time. This skill keeps the discipline and swaps the opportunities box for forces and the experience map for the switch timeline.

**Limitation to know:** built for product teams discovering opportunities, not for competitive or messaging decisions.

## Rob Fitzpatrick — question hygiene

**Who:** entrepreneur and author of *The Mom Test*, widely used in startup accelerators and university entrepreneurship courses.

**Core idea:** ask questions so good that even someone who loves you can't lie to you. Three rules: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past, not generics or opinions about the future; talk less and listen more.

**What it contributes here**
- **The three kinds of bad data:** compliments, fluff (generics, futures, hypotheticals), and ideas — plus what to do with each: deflect, anchor, dig.
- **Commitment and advancement:** a good conversation ends with a concrete next step, not "thanks, that was helpful." For marketing research: a referral, a reference, permission to quote.
- **A fast check for any question.** The basis of the Mom Test check in the guide reference and several rows of Rubrics B and C.

**Limitation to know:** written for founders validating new ideas; its "keep it casual, don't call it an interview" advice suits early discovery more than structured studies with existing customers, where clear consent and a recorded call are the norm.

## Clozd — win-loss operations

**Who:** a win-loss analysis company that runs buyer interviews for B2B companies, publishes the Win-Loss 101 course and a widely cited question framework (Andrew Peterson), and says it has conducted tens of thousands of buyer interviews.

**Core idea:** win-loss interviews are probing, adaptive conversations with buyers soon after a decision — not scripted surveys.

**What it contributes here**
- **Five principles for question design:** start broad and let the buyer tell the story; cover core topics in every interview; prepare drill-downs for recurring themes; avoid closed-ended and scale questions (mostly *what* and *why*); adapt on the fly.
- **Core topics:** requirements, product impressions, sales experience, pricing and packaging, competitive landscape, buying committee, implementation expectations, decision timeline. In this skill, the decision drivers they surface are the forces at the deciding phase.
- **Timing and length:** reach out within about two weeks of the decision; keep it to 30 minutes or less.
- **Interviews vs. surveys:** if it needs a rating, it belongs in a survey; if it needs a story, it belongs in an interview. The best programs mix both.
- **Practice first:** run a couple of practice interviews internally before talking to a real buyer.
- **Neutral interviewers:** buyers are more candid with a third party than with the rep.

**Limitation to know:** Clozd sells services and software, so its guidance naturally emphasizes scale and third-party interviewers; the principles hold for a team doing a handful in-house.

## Where they agree

- **Past behavior beats opinion.** All five.
- **Stories, not surveys, for the why.** Moesta, Torres, Clozd, Laudi & Suellentrop.
- **Don't pitch.** Fitzpatrick most loudly; all five.
- **Adapt to the participant.** Torres's golden rule; Clozd's fifth principle; Moesta's loose use of the forces as a map rather than a script.
- **Talk to the right people, soon.** Moesta's recent switchers; Laudi's best customers; Clozd's closest-to-the-decision buyer within two weeks.
- **About a dozen per round.** Moesta and Laudi & Suellentrop both land on 10–12.

## Where they differ, and how this skill settles it

- **Asking "why."** Clozd's principles favor *what* and *why* questions; Moesta warns that "why" invites after-the-fact reasons people construct on the spot. **This skill:** "why" only about a specific event already on the timeline ("Why that week?"), never about the decision in general ("Why did you choose us?").
- **How much structure.** Moesta's timeline and Clozd's core topics are structure the interviewer must cover; Torres keeps the guide to a story prompt and lets the story lead. **This skill:** Torres governs how you ask — open with a story and follow it. The timeline is the interviewer's checklist for what the story must reach, never read aloud; Clozd's core topics are asked only where the story didn't get there.
- **Who to interview.** Laudi & Suellentrop study best customers; Moesta warns against only happy customers because the struggle is where the data is. **This skill:** the interview type decides. Best-customer studies use Laudi's criteria; when the decision involves who you're losing, add losses, churns or non-switchers.
- **Recency.** Moesta: switchers within about 30–90 days. Clozd: buyers within two weeks. Laudi & Suellentrop: best customers with enough tenure to show value. **This skill:** win-loss within two weeks; churn as soon as possible; best customers about 3–6 months after switching.
- **Segmentation.** Laudi & Suellentrop and Clozd report by segment; Moesta clusters by pathway. **This skill:** pathways first, then check them against segments.
- **Cadence.** Torres: continuously, weekly. Laudi & Suellentrop and Moesta: a focused round of 10–12. Clozd: a standing program across the pipeline. **This skill:** a study for a decision, a program for ongoing intelligence.
- **Formality.** Fitzpatrick favors casual conversations that don't feel like interviews; Moesta, Clozd and Laudi run scheduled, recorded, consented calls. **This skill:** for existing B2B customers, the formal approach.
