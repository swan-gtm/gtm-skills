## Research questions vs. interview questions

Torres's central point: teams conflate what they want to learn with what they ask. Asked directly, people answer instantly, and the fast answer feels true but often isn't — it's filtered through how they'd like to think they behave. Not lying; just unreliable. Moesta makes the same point from the other side: people confabulate reasons, but they can tell you what happened.

So for each research question, ask: *what specific story would answer this?* Then spend the interview collecting that story, in order, and listening for the answers inside it.

| Research question (never asked) | Story prompt (asked) |
|---|---|
| What triggers teams to replace their current [category] setup? | "Take me back to when you first thought you needed something different. What was going on?" |
| How do buyers compare us with Competitor X? | "Walk me through how you narrowed down your options." |
| What progress do customers get from us now? | "Tell me about the last time you used [product] for [job]. Start from what you were trying to get done." |
| Why do small accounts cancel in year one? | "Take me back to when you first started wondering if [product] was the right fit. What happened that week?" |

**Scope the prompt to the decision.** Torres's example: a team looking for brand-new markets asks about the last time someone did the broad activity; a team working on one part of the product scopes the prompt to that part. Too broad and you never reach the moment you care about; too narrow and you miss the context.

## The anatomy of a guide

```
1. Consent and framing (2 min)
   Recording, purpose, how quotes are used. Documentary framing:
   "I'm trying to reconstruct what actually happened, step by
   step. Small details help. There are no wrong answers, and
   I'm not here to sell anything."

2. Warm-up (2–3 min)
   Their role, their organization, who they work with on [job].
   Rapport, not data.

3. Story prompt (the core)
   One or two prompts that open at the first thought.

4. Timeline probes, by phase
   First thought → passive looking → active looking → deciding
   → onboarding → ongoing use (or the variant for this type).
   Pull specifics: when, who, what they did, what they used,
   what they compared, what nearly stopped them.

5. Force probes
   Used when a force surfaces in the story — not as a block
   of questions.

6. Language harvest (3–5 min)
   How they'd describe it, what they'd search for.

7. Must-cover topics
   Only what must be comparable across interviews (competitors,
   pricing reaction, committee). Ask only if the story didn't
   already cover it.

8. Close (3 min)
   "What should I have asked that I didn't?"
   "Who else should we talk to?"
   Thanks; confirm incentive.

9. Post-interview survey (optional)
   Any rated or multiple-choice items.
```

Include a **research-question map** at the bottom of every guide: which probes answer which research question. If a research question has no probe, the guide has a hole. If a probe answers no research question, cut it.

## The Mom Test check

Run every question against Fitzpatrick's three rules:

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Does the question mention our product, feature or concept? Then it invites them to be nice.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or opinions about the future.** Does it contain "usually," "would you," "do you ever," "how likely"? Rewrite toward a specific past instance.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** Is the question longer than one breath? Does it suggest an answer? Shorten it.

And watch for the three kinds of bad data he names:
- **Compliments** — "That sounds great." Worthless as evidence. Deflect and return to their life.
- **Fluff** — generics ("I always…"), future promises ("I'd definitely…"), hypotheticals ("I might…"). Anchor them to a real instance.
- **Ideas and feature requests** — useful, but dig under them: why do you want that, what would it let you do, how are you coping without it today?

## Best-customer / switch guide

Laudi & Suellentrop's best-customer questions, laid on the full switch timeline.

**Story prompt**
> "I'd love to go back to before you were using [product]. When did you first think you might need something different? What was going on?"

**Before the first thought — the situation**
- What were you using to [job] at the time? Who set it up?
- What was working about it? What wasn't?

**First thought**
- When did it first cross your mind that this had to change? What happened that day or week?
- Who else was there? What did they say?

**Passive looking**
- Before you were really shopping, what did you notice? Where?
- Did anyone mention something they used?

**The event — passive to active**
- What happened that made you start actually looking? (A deadline, a new person, a failure, a growth spurt?)

**Active looking**
- Where did you go first? Who did you ask? (Peer and consultant recommendations are often the real channel.)
- What else did you consider — including keeping what you had, building something, or doing nothing?
- How did you narrow it down? What ruled options out?

**Deciding**
- What made it "today" — what set the deadline?
- What tipped it to [product]?
- What nearly stopped you? Who had to agree? Was anyone against it, and what was their argument?
- What did you have to give up, migrate or relearn?

**Onboarding**
- What did you expect the first few weeks to look like? How did it actually go?
- When did you first feel it was working? What happened?

**Ongoing use**
- What can you do now that you couldn't before? How does that feel? Who notices?
- If [product] disappeared tomorrow, what would you do?

Then the language harvest.

## Win-loss guide

Built on Clozd's five principles: start broad and let the buyer tell their story; cover core topics in every interview; prepare drill-downs for recurring themes; keep questions open-ended, mostly *what* and *why*; adapt on the fly. The story concentrates on active looking and deciding. Target 30 minutes.

**Story prompt (adapted from Clozd's broad openers)**
- What prompted you to evaluate [category] solutions at this time?
- How did you structure and run your evaluation?
- What did you decide, and what happened that settled it? (Clozd's version asks why it was the right decision; this skill asks for the event, for the reason given in `practitioners.md`.)

**Core topics — cover in every interview, in whatever order the story allows**
- *First thought and event:* what started the search; what set the deadline
- *Requirements:* what the solution needed to do; which capabilities mattered most
- *Alternatives:* who else was considered, including staying put; who won (on a loss) or was the strongest alternative (on a win); how clear our difference was
- *Product impressions:* strengths and weaknesses as they saw them
- *Sales experience:* what it was like; how it compared; whether it affected the outcome
- *Pricing and packaging:* first reaction to price; comparison; whether the model fit
- *Buying committee:* who was involved, what role each played, and which force each one carried — the person pushing for change, the person worried about migration, the person defending the old way
- *Onboarding expectations:* what they expected the first 30–90 days to look like
- *Timeline:* what sped it up or slowed it down

**Drill-downs** — prepare one for each theme that recurs in your market. Clozd's example: never "Are integrations important?"; instead "Which integrations matter most to you, and why?" A "why" tied to something they named is fine; see the note on "why" in `practitioners.md`.

**Rating items** go in a short follow-up survey, not the call.

## Churn guide

The switch timeline in reverse. The pull now points toward the alternative, including "nothing."

**Story prompt**
> "Take me back to when you first started wondering if [product] was the right fit. What was happening?"

**First doubt**
- What were you hoping [product] would do when you signed up? When did that start to feel off?
- What happened that week?

**Passive looking**
- Did you notice other options before you were really looking? Where?

**The event — passive to active**
- What made you start actually looking for something else, or decide to stop altogether?
- What did you try first? Did you contact us? What happened?

**Deciding to leave**
- Who made the call? Who else was involved? Did anyone argue to stay?
- What nearly kept you? What would have had to be true for you to stay?

**Leaving and after**
- What are you using now — another tool, a spreadsheet, something you built, nothing?
- How did the move go? What do you miss, if anything?
- Is there a situation where you'd come back?

Listen for the habit and anxiety that kept them as long as they stayed; they mark what retention can still use.

## Non-switcher guide

A timeline that stalled, usually at deciding. Anxiety and habit won.

**Story prompt**
> "You took a look at [category] options recently. Walk me through what got you looking and where you ended up."

**Probes**
- When did you first think about changing? What was going on?
- What made you start looking? What did you look at? How far did you get?
- What happened that made you decide to stay with what you had?
- What worried you about switching? What would you have had to give up or redo?
- Who else had a view? What did they say?
- What would have had to be different for you to switch?

Expect answers like "we didn't have time to migrate," "the person who'd do it left," "what we have is fine for now." Each one is an objection the funnel must dismantle.

## Force probes

Use these when a force surfaces in the story — to deepen it, not to introduce it. A force raised by the participant inside an event is evidence; a force named in answer to "what worried you?" in the abstract is a guess.

**Push**
- Walk me through the day that happened. What did it cost you?
- Who else was frustrated? What did they say?
- What had you already tried?

**Pull**
- When you first saw it, what did you picture yourself doing with it?
- What did you hope would stop happening?
- What did you tell your boss it would do?

**Anxiety**
- You mentioned [worry]. What did you think might go wrong?
- What did you check before committing? Who did you ask?
- Who inside was against it? What was their argument?

**Habit**
- What did you have to stop doing?
- Who built or owned the old way? How did that conversation go?
- What are you still doing the old way?

**Progress — all three dimensions**
- Functional: what does that let you do, or do faster?
- Emotional: how did that feel before, and how does it feel now?
- Social: who notices? What do they say?

## Language harvest

The highest-value questions for messaging. Ask near the end, after the story, so their language isn't shaped by yours.

- How would you describe [product] to a colleague who'd never heard of it?
- If you had to explain it to your boss in one sentence, what would you say?
- What would you type into a search box to find something like it?
- What did you call the problem before you had a name for the solution?

Record the answers verbatim. Rubric D codes them into the language inventory: problem nouns, workaround nouns, outcome verbs, search phrases, and the adjectives attached to the pain.

## In the room

**Reconstruct the switch (Moesta & Spiek)**
- **Frame it as a documentary.** You're reconstructing what happened, step by step; small details matter. It lowers anxiety and licenses "and then what happened?"
- **Start at the first thought and go forward.** When they jump ahead, note it and come back: "Let's hold that — before that, what happened?"
- **Anchor on events, not reasons.** "Where were you when you decided?" beats "Why did you decide?" Ask "why" only about a specific event already on the timeline: "Why that week?"
- **Play dumb.** Assume nothing; have them explain the obvious. The obvious is where their language lives.
- **Connect the wrong dots.** Deliberately play back a slightly wrong sequence ("So the renewal came first, and then you started looking?"). People correct a wrong story with the right one, in detail.
- **Treat contradictions as refinements.** When they revise an earlier answer, the second version is usually closer to what happened. Don't point out the inconsistency.
- **Interview in pairs.** One leads the conversation; the other tracks the timeline and the forces, notes gaps, and asks one or two questions at the end.

**Keep it honest (Torres, Fitzpatrick, Clozd)**
- **Open with them, not you.** Never start with a product overview. It turns the interview into a sales call.
- **Stay in the instance.** When they generalize, return: "In that particular case, what did you do?"
- **Get past the first answer.** First answers are often generic or exaggerated. "Can you give me an example?" and "Tell me more about that" get to the specific.
- **Ask for the artifacts.** The spreadsheet, the email to the board, the shortlist. Physical details anchor memory.
- **Use their words.** Repeat their nouns back. Don't introduce your category names.
- **Let silence work.** Wait three seconds after they stop. The second answer is often the real one.
- **Follow energy.** Torres: let the participant talk about what they care about most. Clozd: spend extra time where something was influential; skip what wasn't.
- **Never defend or correct.** If they're wrong about a feature, write it down — the misperception is the finding.
- **Don't take the pitch bait.** If they ask "can it do X?", say you'll follow up after, and ask what X would let them do.
- **Close with the two questions:** what should I have asked, and who else should I talk to.

## Recovery moves

| Situation | Move |
|---|---|
| One-word answers | Switch to a concrete prompt: "Can you walk me through the last time that happened?" |
| They jump straight to "we chose you because…" | "Let's go back further — when did you first think something had to change?" |
| They want to vent about bugs | Let them for a minute, note it, then: "That's helpful. Going back to when you first chose us…" |
| They pitch features | "If you had that, what would it let you do that you can't now?" |
| They compliment the product | "Thanks. Tell me about a time it actually made a difference — what happened?" |
| They can't remember | Use memory cues: "What time of year was it? What else was going on at work?" Or play back a deliberately wrong sequence and let them correct it. If it's still gone, move on — not every interview yields the story you want. |
| They don't have a relevant story | Ask about the nearest one they do have, then end early. Don't force it. |
| The interviewer leads | In debrief, mark the affected section as weak evidence on Rubric C. |

## Questions to retire

| Retire | Why | Replace with |
|---|---|---|
| "What features matter most to you?" | Research question asked aloud | "Tell me about the last time you used it for [job]." |
| "Would you pay $X for…?" | Hypothetical, future | "What did you pay for what you used before? How did you decide it was worth it?" |
| "Do you like our new design?" | About our idea; invites compliments | "Walk me through the last time you [did the task]." |
| "How likely are you to recommend us?" | A rating — belongs in a survey | (Move to post-interview survey) |
| "Why did you choose us?" | Invites a tidy, after-the-fact reason | "Take me back to when you were deciding. What happened?" |
| "What worried you about switching?" (as an opener) | Asks for a force in the abstract | Reach the deciding week first; probe the worry they mention |
| "Don't you think it'd be easier if…?" | Leading | "How do you handle that today?" |
