# The switch model

The one model every part of this skill uses. Every interview type reconstructs a switch, every guide follows its timeline, every rubric scores against it, and every report is organized by it. The model is Bob Moesta's (with Chris Spiek): demand comes from a struggling moment, and a switch happens along a timeline of concrete events, moved by four forces.

This page defines **what** to reconstruct. How to ask for it is in `interview-guide.md`; how to score and code it is in `rubrics.md`; how to find patterns in it is in `synthesis.md`.

## The premise

People don't buy because the features are good. They are trying to make progress in a specific situation, something stands in the way, and at some point a struggling moment turns "this is annoying" into "today's the day." The product gets hired for that progress, and whatever they used before gets fired.

Two consequences shape the whole skill:

1. **Reconstruct events, don't collect opinions.** Ask what happened, in order. Reasons offered after the fact are tidy and unreliable; the sequence of events is not.
2. **The people who moved are the evidence.** Someone who switched — to you, away from you, or nearly — can explain what made moving possible. Someone who never changed anything mostly speculates.

## The timeline

Six phases, separated by events. Every guide's probes are organized by these phases; every coded interview records which phases it reached.

| Phase | What's happening | Capture |
|---|---|---|
| **1. First thought** | The idea that something has to change first enters their head, often weeks or months before any purchase. Usually set off by a specific event. | The date or season, the event, who was there, what they were using then |
| **2. Passive looking** | They notice alternatives but aren't shopping. Low effort; they absorb rather than compare. | What they noticed and where; who mentioned what |
| *Event* | Something escalates and turns noticing into searching. | The escalating event, in their words — this is the moment marketing should meet |
| **3. Active looking** | They spend time: searching, asking peers, building a shortlist, ruling options out. | Where they looked first, who they asked, every option considered including "keep what we have" |
| *Event* | Something sets a deadline or forces a choice. | What made it "today" — a renewal, a new hire, a failure, a board meeting |
| **4. Deciding** | They weigh trade-offs among a few options and commit. | What tipped it, what nearly stopped it, who had to agree, what they gave up |
| **5. Onboarding** | First use, measured against what they expected. | What they expected, what surprised them, when it first felt like it was working |
| **6. Ongoing use** | Regular use for the progress they wanted — or not. | What they can do now that they couldn't before; what they'd do if it disappeared |

Most interviews don't reach every phase cleanly. A strong interview pins at least first thought, the event that started active looking, and deciding to specific moments.

## The four forces

Every switch is a contest:

**Driving change**
- **Push** — frustration with the current situation. What stopped working, and when.
- **Pull** — attraction of the new solution. What they imagined it would let them do.

**Resisting change**
- **Anxiety** — fear of the new. Migration, learning curve, cost of being wrong, what colleagues will think.
- **Habit** — comfort with the present. The current way sort of works; someone built it; people know it.

A switch happens only when push plus pull outweighs anxiety plus habit.

Forces are **heard, not asked.** They surface inside the events of the timeline: the push is in what went wrong the week of the first thought; the anxiety is in what they checked before committing. Asking "what worried you?" in the abstract gets a plausible list. Asking about the week they decided, and then probing the worry they mention, gets the real one.

Teams over-invest in pull — better demos, more features — and leave anxiety and habit untouched. In B2B, habit is the most common invisible competitor, and a process someone built themselves is the hardest habit of all, because their judgment is attached to it.

## Progress: what they were trying to achieve

The progress someone seeks has three dimensions. Code all three; most teams hear only the first.

| Dimension | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| **Functional** | Time, effort, accuracy, capacity — "I needed the reports in an hour, not a day" |
| **Emotional** | How they wanted to feel — "I was tired of dreading month-end" |
| **Social** | How others would see them — "I didn't want to walk into the board meeting with that spreadsheet again" |

The emotional and social dimensions are where messaging gets its edge; the functional dimension is where every competitor already competes.

## Alternatives: what they switched from

The real competitor is whatever they were using before, and it is usually not the one leadership expects. Record every alternative named, from this list:

- Another vendor in the category
- A general-purpose tool bent to the job (a spreadsheet, shared documents, email)
- Something they built themselves
- A person or service doing it by hand
- Nothing — living with the problem

"Nothing" and "build it ourselves" are the alternatives most often missed and most often winning.

## One model, four interview types

Each interview type is the same model viewed from a different angle. Use the same timeline and the same forces; change only where the story concentrates and which way the forces point.

| Type | Stretch of the timeline | Direction of the forces | The question the model answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best-customer / switch** | All six phases; weight on first thought through onboarding | Toward us | What made switching to us possible — so we can find more people in that moment |
| **Win-loss** | Zoomed in on active looking and deciding, across the buying committee | Toward us on a win; toward the alternative on a loss | Which forces decided it, and who on the committee carried each one |
| **Churn** | The timeline run in reverse: first doubt → passive looking at alternatives → active looking → deciding to leave → leaving | Away from us; the pull is toward the alternative, including "nothing" | What pushed them out, and what anxiety and habit kept them as long as they stayed |
| **Non-switcher** | A timeline that stalled, usually at deciding | Anxiety and habit outweighed push and pull | What stops people who wanted to change — the objections the funnel must dismantle |

## Pathways

A **pathway** is a recurring combination of push and pull — a common route people take to a switch. "Outgrew the spreadsheet when a second person joined" plus "wanted something that just worked on day one" is one pathway; "inherited a messy setup" plus "a peer showed them theirs" is another.

Pathways cut across demographic segments. Two organizations of different sizes can share a pathway; two of the same size can take different ones. Cluster interviews by pathway first, then check whether pathways line up with the segments the business already uses. Where they do, the segment is real. Where they don't, the segment is a convenience, and messaging should follow the pathway.
