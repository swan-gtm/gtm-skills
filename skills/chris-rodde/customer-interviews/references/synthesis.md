## Synthesize one at a time

Torres's discipline: synthesize each interview individually, as soon after it as possible. A snapshot written the same day beats a perfect analysis three weeks later, because memory of tone, hesitation and emphasis fades fast. Batch synthesis at the end of a study also makes the last few interviews loom largest.

Order after each interview:
1. Score it (Rubric C) — so you know how much to trust it
2. Write the snapshot — the human summary
3. Code it (Rubric D) — the structured row

## The interview snapshot

Torres's one-page template for a single interview has six parts. Adapted for marketing research:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ [Identifier — role, org type, segment]   Date · Interviewer│
│ "Memorable quote — the line that captures this person"   │
├─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┤
│ Quick facts          │ Insights                          │
│ - Segment, size      │ - Surprising or notable things    │
│ - Prior approach     │   that aren't opportunities      │
│ - Alternatives       │                                   │
│ - Type & evidence wt │                                   │
├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
│ Forces               │ Switch timeline                   │
│ - Push / pull /      │ First thought → passive → event → │
│   anxiety / habit,   │ active → deciding → onboarding →  │
│   in their words     │ ongoing use (or the variant for   │
│ - Pathway            │ this interview type), with dates  │
└─────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┘
```

Torres's original has an "opportunities" box for needs, pain points and desires; in this skill those are the forces and progress, coded in the model's vocabulary.

Torres uses a photo to make the person memorable; for B2B marketing research, a short persona line (role, org type, situation) often does the same job and avoids storing images.

## The coding sheet

One row per interview, columns from Rubric D. Keep it in a shared sheet so everyone sees the same evidence. Add filter columns for segment, type and evidence weight.

## Finding patterns: one frame, three views

Work in this order. Each view uses the same coded rows; none needs its own coding pass.

### 1. Pathways — who switches, and by what route (Moesta)

Sort the coded rows by their pathway field — the dominant push plus the dominant pull. Merge pathways that are the same route in different words; name each in customer language; count interviews per pathway.

Then cross pathways against the segments the business already uses (size, plan, tenure, vertical):

| Pathway | Interviews | Segments it appears in | Typical first thought | Typical event to active looking |
|---|---|---|---|---|

- A pathway concentrated in one segment confirms that segment as real.
- A pathway spread across segments means messaging should follow the pathway, not the segment.
- A segment split across several pathways is a convenience, not a market.

### 2. Forces and progress by pathway — what to say and what to dismantle (Moesta, Laudi & Suellentrop)

Within each pathway, average force strength (Rubric D) and cluster the verbatims under each force. This is where Laudi & Suellentrop's theme work lives: cluster quotes that say the same thing in different words, name the theme in customer language, and count interviews. Their struggle themes are the push column; motivation is pull; desired outcome is progress.

| Pathway | Push themes | Pull themes | Anxiety themes | Habit themes | Progress sought (functional / emotional / social) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Read it for marketing:
- **Push** themes become the problem statement — the struggling moment in their words.
- **Pull** and **progress** themes become the value proposition. The emotional and social columns are usually where the edge is.
- **Anxiety** themes become objection handling, proof and risk reversal.
- **Habit** themes become migration and onboarding messaging.

Then lay the themes on the switch timeline. Laudi & Suellentrop's experience map — struggle, evaluation, growth — is the same timeline at lower resolution. Mark where each force peaks and which milestones a customer must reach in onboarding to get the progress they came for. That map shows where messaging, onboarding and retention work should land.

### 3. Forces at deciding — what wins and loses deals (Clozd)

For win-loss, the decision drivers are the forces at the deciding phase. Tally the top one per interview by outcome:

| Force at deciding | Wins citing | Losses citing | Most-named alternative | Committee role that carried it |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Look for forces that appear in losses but not wins — that is where the deal was decided. Compare buyer-stated reasons with recorded loss reasons; the gap is itself a finding.

### What to look for in every study
- **The real alternative.** Usually not the competitor leadership expects — often a spreadsheet, something built in-house, or nothing.
- **The event to active looking.** What turned noticing into searching. It's the moment marketing should meet.
- **Time from first thought to deciding.** Long gaps mean habit is strong and there is time to be found; short gaps mean being findable at the event matters most.
- **The real channel.** How they first heard — often a peer or consultant.
- **The customer's category word.** What they'd type into a search box.
- **The anxiety that nearly stopped them.** Objection-handling material.
- **Surprises.** Anything that contradicts current belief is worth more than confirmation.

## The evidence standard

- **Report frequency honestly.** "7 of 11 best customers named a spreadsheet as their prior approach." Never "most customers" without a count.
- **Separate observed from inferred.** Findings say what was heard; implications say what it might mean. Label them.
- **Weight by quality.** A finding needs at least three interviews in the pathway or segment, at least two strong on Rubric C.
- **Quote with clearance.** Role and organization type by default; names only with permission.
- **State what it rules out.** The most useful line in many reports: "No interviewee cited price as a reason for leaving."
- **Note the sample's limits.** Who wasn't interviewed, and how that might bias the result.

## Hand-off formats

### For messaging and positioning (the evidence input to a positioning decision)

```
# Demand brief — [date, N interviews, segments]

## The struggling moment
One paragraph in customer language: the concrete moment that
starts the search, and the event that turns it into active looking.

## Four Forces
Push / Pull / Anxiety / Habit — ranked, with verbatims and counts
Anxiety → objection handling; habit → migration and onboarding

## Progress
Functional / emotional / social, in their words

## Language inventory
Problem nouns, workaround nouns, outcome verbs, search phrases,
pain adjectives — verbatim
Words only we use (candidates for retirement)

## Pathways and segments
The recurring routes to a switch, with counts; where forces differ
materially by pathway or segment

## What this rules out
Claims the evidence does not support

## Evidence quality
Interviews by evidence weight; gaps in the sample
```

### For win-loss

```
# Win-loss findings — [period, N wins, N losses]

## Headline
The one thing most likely to change win rate

## Forces at deciding
Table by outcome, with verbatims and the committee role that
carried each force

## Competitor patterns
Who we lose to — including staying put — on what, in which
pathway or segment

## Sales-process findings
What buyers said about the experience

## Pricing perception
How price was received and compared

## Recommendations
Owner and action per finding
```

### For churn

```
# Churn findings — [period, N interviews, segments]

## Pathways out
The recurring routes away from us — push away, pull toward the
alternative — ranked, with force strength and verbatims

## Where they went
Competitor, general-purpose tool, something built, nothing

## The reversed timeline
First doubt → passive looking → the event → deciding to leave.
Where on that timeline someone could have intervened, and what
habit or anxiety kept them as long as they stayed

## Within our control / outside it
Split the reasons honestly

## Recommendations
```

Every report ends by answering the decision sentence from the research brief.

## Common mistakes

- **Batch synthesis three weeks later.** The nuance is gone.
- **Counting mentions instead of interviews.** One person saying "price" five times is one interview.
- **Paraphrased "quotes."** If it's in quotation marks, it's verbatim.
- **Themes named in house language.** Name them in the customer's words.
- **Two parallel analyses.** A themes table and a forces table built separately, with different counts. Code once; read the same rows through each view.
- **Segmenting by demographics first.** Start from pathways, then check them against segments — not the other way round.
- **Confirmation hunting.** Look hardest for evidence against the current belief.
- **No answer to the decision.** A themes deck that changes nothing.
