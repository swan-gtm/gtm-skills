# Customer interviews for B2B marketing

Interviews are the evidence under positioning, messaging, ICP and growth decisions. Most interview programs fail in one of three places: they talk to the wrong people, they ask questions people can't answer truthfully, or the findings never become a decision. This skill prevents all three.

## One model, five roles

Every interview in this skill reconstructs the same thing: a **switch**. Bob Moesta's model (with Chris Spiek) defines it. A switch moves along a timeline of concrete events — first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, onboarding, ongoing use — and happens only when **push** (frustration with the present) plus **pull** (attraction of the new) outweighs **anxiety** (fear of the new) plus **habit** (comfort with the present). Best-customer, win-loss, churn and non-switcher interviews are that model seen from different angles.

The other practitioners each do one job inside the model:

| Practitioner | Their job here |
|---|---|

## The sequence

| Step | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Decide | What decision will this research inform? | Research brief |
| 2. Select | Who has the evidence, and how do we reach them? | Participant criteria, screener, invite |
| 3. Design | What switch story do we collect to answer our research questions? | Interview guide |
| 4. Run | Are we reconstructing events, or collecting polite opinions? | Recordings, transcripts |
| 5. Score | Was each interview reliable, and what did it say? | Quality score, coded transcript, snapshot |
| 6. Synthesize | What pathways and forces hold across interviews, and what do they rule out? | Findings report or demand brief |

Announce the step. Each step narrows the next. A request that starts at step 3 ("give me interview questions") almost always hides an unstated decision and unchosen participants; ask the step 1 and 2 questions briefly first unless the user has answered them.

## Step 1 — Decide

Read `references/study-design.md`. Get one sentence: **"We are deciding ___, and we'll know enough when ___."** Then choose the interview type — best-customer, win-loss, churn or non-switcher — from the variants in the switch model, and write 3–5 **research questions**. Research questions go in the brief and are never asked aloud; interview questions collect the story that answers them.

## Step 2 — Select

Read `references/study-design.md`. Interview people who moved — to you, away from you, or nearly — while the timeline is fresh. For best-customer studies, use Laudi & Suellentrop's criteria; for win-loss, reach the person closest to the decision within about two weeks. Plan 10–12 interviews per segment; Moesta and Laudi & Suellentrop independently land on the same number. Draft the screener and invite from the templates. Never pitch in the invite.

## Step 3 — Design the guide

Read `references/interview-guide.md`. A guide is one or two story prompts plus probes organized by the phases of the switch timeline. Translate each research question into the story that answers it; lay the probes on the timeline; add Clozd-style must-cover topics only where interviews must be comparable; move ratings to a post-interview survey; run every question through the Mom Test check; and include the language-harvest questions. Target 30–45 minutes for customers, 30 or less for win-loss buyers. Score the draft with Rubric B before fielding.

## Step 4 — Run

Read "In the room" in `references/interview-guide.md`. Frame the conversation as a documentary: you're reconstructing what happened, and small details matter. Start at the first thought and move forward in order, asking "and then what happened?" Stay in the specific instance. Let forces surface from events, then probe them. Interview in pairs where possible — one leads, one tracks the timeline. Record and transcribe; the exact words are the asset.

## Step 5 — Score

Read `references/rubrics.md`. Four rubrics on anchored 1–4 scales: **A** participant fit (before scheduling), **B** guide quality (before the first interview), **C** interview quality (after each interview — how much its evidence counts), **D** evidence coding (after each interview — timeline, forces, progress, alternatives, language). Tailor any rubric to the user's market before delivering it; a generic rubric is a starting point, not a deliverable.

## Step 6 — Synthesize

Read `references/synthesis.md`. Snapshot and code each interview the same day. Then cluster interviews by **pathway** — recurring combinations of push and pull — and check pathways against the segments the business already uses. Report forces by pathway with honest counts, state what the evidence rules out, and hand off in the format the decision needs: a demand brief for messaging, decision drivers for win-loss, a reversed-timeline reason map for churn.

## What good looks like

- **The decision comes before the questions.** A study with a named decision and an evidence threshold produces findings someone acts on; one that starts with "give me interview questions" produces a themes deck nobody uses.
- **Transcripts read like a documentary, not a survey.** Dates, names, the spreadsheet they used before, the meeting where someone pushed back. If most answers start with "usually" or "I think," the interviewer asked for opinions and the evidence is weak.
- **The timeline has anchors.** A strong interview pins first thought, the event that started active looking, and the decision to specific moments. A weak one jumps from "we needed something" to "we chose you."
- **Anxiety and habit show up in the coding.** The mediocre study captures push and pull only. The good one names what nearly stopped the switch, because that is what the funnel and the copy must dismantle.
- **"Nothing" and "build it ourselves" appear as alternatives.** If every interview names only category competitors, the interviewer didn't ask what they did before.
- **Pathways, not demographics, organize the findings.** A handful of recurring routes to a switch tells marketing where to meet buyers; a breakdown by company size usually doesn't.
- **Findings carry counts and limits.** "7 of 11 delayed over migration" beats "customers worry about migration." Every finding names the pathway or segment it holds for and what it rules out.
- **The participant does nearly all the talking.** Interviewer talk time above about 30% almost always means pitching, leading or answering for them.
- **Verbatims survive intact.** The customer's word for the problem is the copy.

## Rules

- MUST name the decision the study informs before drafting any interview question.
- MUST keep research questions out of the interview; collect the story that answers them.
- MUST reconstruct events in order; NEVER ask "why did you choose us?" or other reasons-in-general questions. "Why" is allowed only about a specific event already on the timeline.
- MUST interview people who moved — including losses, churns or non-switchers whenever the decision involves who you're losing. NEVER sample only champions or the most responsive contacts.
- MUST use a neutral interviewer for win-loss and churn where possible, and record who interviewed.
- MUST get recording consent, store transcripts where participants were told, and anonymize quotes by default.
- MUST score each interview before its evidence carries a finding; an interview scoring 1 on story specificity or neutrality never carries a finding alone.
- NEVER ask hypotheticals ("would you…", "how much would you pay…") or pitch the product during an interview.
- NEVER paraphrase inside quotation marks, or name themes in house language.
- NEVER report a pattern from a single interview, however vivid.
- MUST end every report by answering the decision sentence from step 1.

## Output templates

### Research brief (end of step 1)
```
Decision this informs — one sentence
We'll know enough when — the evidence threshold
Interview type — best-customer / win-loss / churn / non-switcher
Research questions — 3–5, never asked aloud
Segments — and target interviews per segment
Out of scope — what this study will not answer
Owner, interviewer(s), dates
```

### Interview guide (end of step 3)
```
Header — study, interview type, length, interviewers, consent script
Warm-up — role and context (2–3 min)
Story prompt — the one or two questions that open the story
Timeline probes — by phase of the switch timeline
Force probes — to use when a force surfaces
Language harvest — how they'd describe it, what they'd search
Must-cover topics — only where interviews must be comparable
Close — what we didn't ask, who else to talk to, thanks
Post-interview survey — any rated items
Research-question map — which probes answer which research question
```
