# The six retired patterns

These patterns are common enough in machine-written marketing copy that a reader's ear picks them up within a paragraph. Each has a test, a fix, and a note on when it is acceptable. Treat them as firm rules when editing; the fixes keep the meaning and change only the construction.

## 1. The fragment with a tag

**What it is.** A noun phrase with no verb, followed by a comma and a trailing participle, a second noun phrase or a colon list. It reads as a slogan where a sentence should be.

**Test.** Remove the punctuation and ask who does what. If there is no subject doing something, it is a fragment.

- Retire: "Four layers, built in order." / "A rhythm your team can hold: long pieces on your own site, short posts, and nurture." / "The claim only you can make, drawn from your closed deals."
- Fix: "The stack has four layers, and each one rests on the layer beneath it." / "Publishing compounds when your team can sustain it, which in practice means a long piece on your own site, short posts and nurture email." / "Position is the claim only you can make, and it comes from your closed deals."

Short labels of one to three words (a layer name on a card) are the only exception. The same family covers `N nouns, N nouns` pairings ("Two teams, one goal") and "X, not Y" contrasts.

## 2. The "so" splice

**What it is.** A clause, then "so" and a second clause, with or without the comma. This includes ", so that" and the bare form "put in place so the team can act".

**Test.** Does "so" join two full clauses? If so, rewrite.

- Retire: "Definition pages that explain the category properly, so the first thing a buyer reads is yours."
- Fix with the effect as the main verb: "Definition pages that explain the category properly make yours the first thing a buyer reads."
- Fix with the condition first: "When one-pagers, decks and follow-ups draw on the same argument, a conversation picks up where the reading stopped."
- Fix with "because" when a reason is meant: "Large deals involve several people who rarely want the same thing, because each is measured on something different."
- Fix with an infinitive for purpose: "to give the whole buying group one consistent story".

"So" as an intensifier ("so many") is fine. "So" as a sentence opener is not.

## 3. "Worth" as an editorial nudge

**What it is.** Phrases that point at importance without showing any: "worth knowing", "worth noting", "worth considering", "worth watching", "worth a look", "worth acting on". Relatives include "it is important to note", "it bears mentioning", "keep in mind", "here's the thing", "the truth is", "the reality is" and "interestingly".

**Test.** Delete the phrase. If the sentence still stands, the phrase was filler. If it falls over, the sentence never made its point.

- Retire: "The part worth internalising is that buyers no longer start from a blank page."
- Fix: "Buyers no longer start from a blank page, which changes what content is for."

A literal money value is fine ("an estimated $78 billion", "worth $5 million").

## 4. "Long before" and "long after"

**What it is.** "long before", "long after", "long since", "well before", "well after". They gesture at a span of time without giving it.

**Test.** Can you name the interval or the event? Say it.

- Retire: "Your market knows what you stand for long before it speaks to your sales team."
- Fix: "Your market knows what you stand for months before anyone books a call." Or with evidence: "Between 80 and 90 percent of buyers had a shortlist before formal research began."

## 5. The "none of it" closer

**What it is.** A sweeping negative tacked onto a list or claim: "none of it", "none of them", "none of that", "none of those".

**Test.** What is the actual positive fact behind the negative? State that.

- Retire: "None of that makes the decision less human."
- Fix with the positive: "The decision is still made by people, each with their own worries and their own career at stake."
- Fix with a precise negative: "No individual in the group can say yes alone."

"None of the three" is fine when it answers a question of choice. Legal copy may keep "none of" where exact wording matters.

## 6. The sentence that announces its point

**What it is.** A sentence built to name its subject before saying anything about it, usually with a noun phrase such as "the part", "the thing", "the key", "the first job" or "the difference" followed by "is" and a gerund. The family includes "What matters is", "What separates X from Y is" and the debunking opener "Most of what gets called X is Y".

**Test.** Does the first half say anything the reader could not have guessed? If not, delete the frame and start with the claim.

- Retire: "The part that compounds is deciding what you are willing to say that your competitors are not."
- Fix with the actor doing the thing: "Content compounds when a company decides what it is willing to say that its competitors are not."
- Retire: "Most of what gets called SEO today still means chasing a ranking."
- Fix: "SEO today still often means chasing a ranking."

A plain noun phrase as a subject is fine when it names something concrete ("Dependence is the main risk"). The related contrast "is not X, it is Y" belongs to the contrast family in `house-style-options.md`.

## When two fixes collide

Fixing one pattern often creates another. A "so" splice rewritten as ", which" three times in a paragraph is its own tell, and a fragment glued to the next sentence with a colon is still a fragment. Vary the repair (effect verb, condition first, "because", an infinitive) and reread the whole paragraph once the fixes are in.
