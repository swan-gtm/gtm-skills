# Voice and structure

The target voice is research-led advisory writing: a senior practitioner explaining a finding to a peer, with the claim stated first and the evidence doing the persuading. Authoritative without being loud, plain without being thin, specific wherever it can be.

## The voice

| Quality | What it looks like on the page |
|---|---|
| Claim first | The sentence a reader most needs comes first in the page, the section and the paragraph. |
| Evidence carries the weight | A figure arrives with its unit, source and date. Assumptions are stated before the arithmetic that depends on them. |
| Calibrated confidence | Sourced facts are flat. Forecasts and judgments take "could", "may" or "tends to". |
| Plain vocabulary | The word a client would use in a meeting, and the specific thing instead of its category. |
| Honest limits | Each argument admits what still has to be true for it to work. |
| People as subjects | Companies, buyers, teams and investors do things. Abstractions rarely do. |

Address the reader as "you" when the page is about their situation, and use "we" for the company's own work, research and position.

## Structural moves to use

1. **Open on the gap.** State what is commonly believed or what has been achieved, pivot with "But", "Yet" or "However", and name the more useful question.
2. **Headings make a claim or ask a plain question,** in sentence case.
3. **Sections follow a steady arc.** The problem with a number in it, why it persists, what changes it, who is already doing it, and what still has to be true.
4. **Walk through the evidence.** State the assumption, show the arithmetic, land on the gap.
5. **Bold lead-in, then elaboration.** A list item begins with a bold sentence that makes a complete claim, and the following sentences support it. A bare label is acceptable for one to three words.
6. **Name names.** A named company, product, outcome and year does more than a statement about "leading organizations".
7. **State the evidence base up front** (how many interviews, which period, which sample), which tells the reader what the claims rest on.
8. **End on the next decision.** Close on the open question or the choice that now faces the reader. A slogan is not a close.

## Sentence craft

**Subject and finite verb.** Every sentence in body copy has one. That covers paragraphs, card descriptions, list items, hero sub-lines and FAQ answers.

**Length.** Aim for roughly 15 to 30 words, with a ceiling near 40. A sentence that has collected a list of three items and two further clauses should be split at the clause boundary into two complete sentences, never chopped into fragments. Never stack more than two sentences in a row under eight words.

**Connectors do the logical work, and they vary.** "Because" for a reason, "while" and "although" for a contrast, "when", "once" and "if" for a condition, "but" and "yet" for a turn, and "which" for a consequence or comment, at most once a paragraph. Putting the condition first often removes the need for a connector: "When one-pagers and decks come from the same argument, a conversation picks up where the reading stopped."

**Let the verb carry the cause.** Verbs such as keeps, lets, gives, means, makes and puts connect an action to its effect without a splice. "Nurture email keeps you familiar between deals" needs nothing added.

**Vary openers.** No more than two consecutive sentences should begin with the same word.

**Read it aloud once.** If it sounds like a list of statements or a slogan stack, rewrite it as continuous prose.
