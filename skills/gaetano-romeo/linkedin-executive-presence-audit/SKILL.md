Judge whether an executive's LinkedIn activity builds authority with the audience that matters, rather than reach among peers.

## Inputs to load

* The profile headline, about section and featured items.
* The last 20 to 30 posts with their reactions, comments and saves.
* The business goal behind the presence: pipeline, hiring, partnerships or category positioning.
* The target audience, described by role and company type.

## Run five tests

1. **Positioning clarity.** A stranger must be able to say in one sentence what this person is known for after reading the headline and the last five posts.
2. **Hook strength.** Read only the first two lines of each post. Weak hooks warm up, announce or thank. Strong hooks state a claim, a result or a tension.
3. **Theme concentration.** Count distinct themes. More than three means the audience cannot learn what to expect.
4. **Proof density.** Split posts into own data and own experience versus borrowed ideas and generic advice. Authority comes from the first group.
5. **Audience quality.** Sample who comments and who saves. Estimate the share of engagement coming from the target audience versus peers, recruiters and engagement groups.

## Check the conversion path

Follow one reader from a post to the profile to a next step. If the profile does not tell a buyer, candidate or partner what to do next, the content cannot convert, however well it performs.

## Verdict

* **Authority building:** clear positioning, concentrated themes, high proof density, engagement from the target audience.
* **Visible but generic:** reach is healthy, proof density is low, engagement comes mostly from peers.
* **Active but unfocused:** strong individual posts across too many themes.
* **Invisible:** unclear positioning, inconsistent cadence, weak hooks.

Each verdict ends with the single change that moves the profile up one level.

## What good looks like

* **The expert's tell:** the ratio of own proof to borrowed ideas, and the quality of the first line. Both predict authority better than impressions.
* **The common mistake:** optimizing for reach among peers while the buyers never see or trust the content. Generic motivation, long warm ups and announcement posts are the usual symptoms.
* **A good output** gives one clear verdict, three prioritized changes with expected effect, and a proposed theme set of no more than three.
* **Engagement is read by who, not how many.** A smaller audience of the right buyers beats a large audience of peers.

## Hard rules

* NEVER invent metrics or infer private analytics that were not provided.
* NEVER post, comment or message from the executive's account. Output is a recommendation reviewed by a human.
* ALWAYS express performance as percentages and ratios, not raw counts, unless absolute numbers are requested.
* ALWAYS flag when the sample is too small to support a verdict.
