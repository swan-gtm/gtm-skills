# Worked examples

Three illustrative cases. They are composites written to show the judgment, not records of specific accounts.

## 1. The template that failed the swap test

A first draft for a regional grocery chain opened with "Grocery is changing fast" and offered three hypotheses about margin pressure, labor costs and digital adoption. Swapped for the chain's largest competitor, every block still read correctly. It was a segment page with one name changed.

A second research pass found the chain's chief executive describing, in an earnings call, a goal to cut out-of-stocks in fresh categories, and eight new demand-planning roles posted in two months. The rewrite led with the quote, built one hypothesis around fresh-category forecasting, and dropped the other two. The page got shorter and stronger.

**The judgment:** fewer, sharper hypotheses beat broader coverage. If the research does not support three, use one.

## 2. The hypothesis stated as fact

A page for a software company's operations leader stated: "Your support team is overwhelmed and churn is rising." The research behind it was a cluster of support job postings and some negative reviews. Read by the operations leader, the line was presumptuous at best and wrong at worst; churn figures were not public.

The rewrite: "You have opened twelve support roles this year, most of them for the same product line. Hiring at that pace usually means ticket volume is growing faster than the knowledge base behind it." Same evidence, framed as a pattern the reader could confirm or correct. The reply corrected one detail and agreed with the rest, which was the opening for a meeting.

**The judgment:** an outsider's certainty about an account's internal problems reads as presumption. A sourced pattern reads as expertise.

## 3. The account that did not earn a microsite

A team asked for microsites for 40 accounts in a new segment. The research for the first five produced only generic findings: no executive statements, no hiring clusters, no recent events. Every draft failed the swap test.

The recommendation was to stop at the accounts where public evidence supported a specific point of view (two of the five), send those pages, and run a sharp segment-level email for the rest. The two pages produced one meeting; the pages that could not pass the swap test would likely have produced none and cost the team its credibility with those readers.

**The judgment:** whether the account deserves a microsite depends on the evidence available as much as on its tier. When public evidence is thin, the page cannot be personal, and a page that is not personal does more harm than a good email.
