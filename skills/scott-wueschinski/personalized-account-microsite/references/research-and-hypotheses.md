# Research and hypotheses

## Sources, in order of value

| Source | What it tells you | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| The executive's own words (earnings calls, interviews, conference talks, posts) | Stated priorities, the language they use, what they are measured on | Quote briefly and accurately, with a link. The single most persuasive element on the page |
| Annual report, investor materials, strategy announcements | Stated goals, named initiatives, risks they disclose | Anchor the point of view to a goal they have already committed to publicly |
| Job postings | Where money is going now: new teams, new tools, new regions | Count roles by function and location; a cluster is a signal, a single posting is noise |
| Press releases and news | Acquisitions, launches, leadership changes, expansions | Use for "why now"; an event under a year old carries more weight |
| Product, pricing and careers pages | How they sell, what they charge, how they describe themselves | Mirror their vocabulary on the page |
| Review sites and community forums | What their customers and employees complain about | Use carefully and only in aggregate; never quote an individual reviewer |
| Technology footprint | Platforms they run | Use only when it bears on the point of view |

Collect at least five facts. Record for each: the fact, the date, the link, and the priority it suggests.

## What stays out

- Anything not publicly available, including information from a former employee or a shared contact, unless that person has agreed to its use.
- Personal details about any individual beyond their professional role and public professional statements.
- Speculation about layoffs, finances or legal matters beyond what the company has disclosed.

## Writing a hypothesis

A hypothesis has three parts: the observed fact, the inferred priority, and the cost of that priority going unmet.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Retailers are under margin pressure." | "Your last two quarterly calls named shrink as a top-three margin driver, and you have opened four loss-prevention analyst roles since. Programs at that stage usually stall on data that arrives too late to act on." |
| "You are growing fast." | "You opened three regional offices this year and list nine sales operations roles. Territory and routing rules written for one region rarely survive the third." |
| "AI is a priority for you." | "Your chief operating officer told an industry conference that every service process will have an automated first pass within two years. Most service teams find their knowledge base, not the model, is the constraint." |

Rules for hypotheses:

- Each one must fail the swap test: it should be wrong for a competitor.
- Use two or three. One reads as a guess; five read as a lecture.
- Phrase them so the reader can correct them: "usually means", "most teams at this stage find". A corrected hypothesis is a conversation.
- Rank them by how directly they connect to the point of view, and lead with the strongest.

## Choosing the point of view

The point of view is the one change the page argues for. Test it against three questions:

1. Does it follow from the strongest hypothesis?
2. Is it something your offer can actually deliver in a first phase?
3. Would the reader's own leadership agree it matters, based on what they have said publicly?

If any answer is no, pick a different hypothesis to lead with, or reconsider whether this account is ready for a one-to-one play.
