Applies to one-to-one account plays where the account justifies a few hours of work: a top-tier target, a stalled enterprise opportunity, or a named executive the team has not been able to reach. Produces a page draft (structure, copy and sources) and the short message that delivers the link.

A microsite works for one reason: it is proof of effort that the reader can verify in ten seconds. It fails the moment the reader suspects it is a template with their logo dropped in. This skill's core rule: **every personalized claim on the page must be specific, sourced, and wrong for any other company.** If a sentence would still read correctly after swapping in a competitor's name, it does not belong on the page.

## Build the page

1. **Confirm the account earns it.** A microsite is for accounts in the top tier of the target list, or for one named stakeholder in a live pursuit. For anything broader, a sharp email does the job at a fraction of the effort. Agree on the single reader (one executive, or one buying committee with a named lead) before research starts.
2. **Research from public sources only.** Annual reports and earnings commentary, press releases, job postings, executive interviews and posts, product and pricing pages, review sites, and technology footprint. Collect at least five dated, linkable facts. The source list and how to read each one are in `references/research-and-hypotheses.md`.
3. **Write two or three hypotheses, not conclusions.** Each pairs an observed fact with an inferred priority and the cost of that priority going unmet: "You are hiring eleven data engineers across three regions. That usually means a consolidation effort is under way, and consolidation is where reporting breaks." Phrase them as hypotheses the reader can correct. An outsider stating an account's internal problems as fact reads as presumptuous; a well-sourced hypothesis reads as informed.
4. **Pick one point of view.** The page argues one thing: the specific change the account should make, and why now. A page that covers every capability argues nothing.
5. **Draft the page to the blueprint.** Six blocks, one screen of reading each at most: the reader's situation, the hypotheses, the point of view, relevant proof, what a first step looks like, one call to action. The blueprint, copy rules and design constraints are in `references/page-blueprint.md`.
6. **Run the swap test.** Replace the account's name with a direct competitor's and reread every block. Any sentence that still works is generic: rewrite it with an account-specific fact or cut it.
7. **Write the delivery message.** Three to five sentences: the single most specific observation, the fact that the page exists and is built for them, and the link. The message must stand on its own; a reader who never clicks should still learn something.
8. **Hand off for review.** A person reviews the page and the message, checks every source link, and publishes and sends.

## What good looks like

- **What the best operator notices first:** the account's own words. An executive's quote from an earnings call or interview, reflected back accurately with a link, does more than any amount of design. It proves listening, and it is impossible to template.
- **The common mistake:** personalization by substitution: the logo, the company name in the headline, the industry in the subhead, and a generic page underneath. Readers recognize it at once, and it costs more credibility than a plain email would have.
- **The second mistake:** too much page. A microsite that tries to be a website gets skimmed and closed. One argument, one proof point that matches, one ask.
- **How you know the output is good:** it passes the swap test on every block; every factual claim links to a public source; hypotheses are phrased so the reader can correct them; there is exactly one call to action; and the delivery message would earn a reply without the link. Worked cases are in `references/worked-examples.md`.

## Rules

- MUST use only public sources, and link every factual claim on the page to one.
- MUST phrase inferences about the account's internal priorities as hypotheses, never as facts.
- MUST pass the swap test on every block before handoff.
- NEVER include personal information about an individual beyond their public professional role and public statements.
- NEVER add tracking, forms or scripts that send visitor data anywhere other than the team's own analytics and systems.
- NEVER publish the page or send the message; produce drafts for a person to review, publish and send.
