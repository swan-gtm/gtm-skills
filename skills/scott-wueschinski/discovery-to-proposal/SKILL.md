Applies after one or more discovery conversations, when the buyer has asked for a proposal or the seller is about to send one. Produces either a discovery gap list (when the deal is not ready) or a proposal draft plus a one-page summary the champion can forward unchanged.

A proposal does not persuade; discovery does. The proposal's job is to write down what the buyer already agreed to, in their words, so they can sell it internally when the seller is not in the room. Its core rule: **nothing appears in the proposal that the buyer has not already heard and confirmed.** A proposal that introduces a new number, scope item or price is a first draft of a negotiation, and it stalls.

## Build the proposal

1. **Test discovery before writing a word.** Score the deal on six elements: confirmed problem, quantified impact, economic buyer, decision process and date, success measures, and the cost of doing nothing. Each element is met only when the call notes contain the buyer's own statement of it. If two or more are missing, stop and return the gap list with the question that closes each one. The rubric and the questions are in `references/discovery-sufficiency.md`.
2. **Extract the buyer's language.** Pull direct quotes for the problem, the impact and the desired outcome. These become the opening section verbatim or close to it. A buyer who reads their own words on page one keeps reading.
3. **Size the value from their numbers, never yours.** Rebuild the impact figure from the inputs the buyer gave (volumes, rates, hours, revenue at risk), show the arithmetic, and state every assumption. When an input is missing, show a range and name who confirms it. Price at no more than one fifth of first-year quantified impact; past that, the business case needs a second source of value or a smaller first phase.
4. **Write three options.** A focused option that solves the confirmed problem, a recommended option that adds the next most valuable outcome, and a broader option that shows the full path. Price each from scope, not from discount. The structure and pricing logic are in `references/proposal-structure.md`.
5. **Write the mutual plan to signature.** Every step from proposal review to kickoff, with an owner on each side and a date, ending at the decision date the buyer gave. A step with no buyer-side owner is a guess.
6. **Name the risks and who owns them.** Data access, internal resourcing, competing priorities, procurement. A buyer trusts a seller who names the risk before they do.
7. **Write the one-page summary last.** Problem, value, recommended option, price, decision date. It is what the champion forwards to the economic buyer, so it must stand alone.
8. **Review against discovery.** Mark every claim with the call or note it came from. Anything unsourced is either confirmed with the buyer before sending or cut.

## What good looks like

- **What the best operator notices first:** whether the economic buyer has said the impact number out loud. A champion's estimate of impact gets challenged in the approval meeting; the economic buyer's own estimate does not.
- **The common mistake:** writing a capability document. Mediocre proposals open with the seller's company, list features, and put price on the last page. Good ones open with the buyer's problem in the buyer's words, put value before price, and could be presented by the champion without the seller.
- **The second mistake:** writing the proposal to close a gap in discovery. A proposal sent to "see what they think about price" teaches the buyer to negotiate rather than decide.
- **How you know the output is good:** every number traces to a buyer statement or a stated assumption; the recommended option's price is under one fifth of first-year impact; the mutual plan has a buyer owner on every step; and the champion could forward the one-page summary unchanged. Worked cases, including one that should not have been written, are in `references/worked-examples.md`.

## Rules

- MUST stop and return the gap list when two or more discovery elements are missing.
- MUST source every figure to a buyer statement or a labeled assumption.
- MUST present three priced options, with one marked as recommended and the reason given.
- NEVER introduce scope, price or terms the buyer has not heard in conversation.
- NEVER send, share or upload the proposal; produce the draft for a person to review and send.
- NEVER place confidential information from one buyer into another buyer's proposal, including as an anonymized example, without that customer's permission.
